# Scientific limitations

CardioSim Lab is an educational, reduced-order model. It is **not** clinically validated and must not be used for diagnosis or patient-specific prediction. This file lists what is mechanistic, what is reduced-order, and where the output is known to deviate from human data.

## What is governed by state equations and what is schematic

| Layer | Implementation | Class |
|---|---|---|
| Ventricular single cell (Ionic cell tab) | ten Tusscher–Panfilov 2006 (TP06), 19 state variables, Rush–Larsen, dt 0.02 ms | **mechanistic (ionic ODEs)** |
| Tissue action potentials (all views on the main clock) | Reduced-order waveform; APD set per cell from a restitution map (Mitchell–Schaeffer fit) | reduced-order |
| Activation | Event-driven graph propagation on a 2.5 mm voxel grid; anisotropy, refractoriness, CV restitution | reduced-order (mechanistic sequence, no source–sink) |
| AV node, slow pathway, accessory pathways | Lumped directional links with a recovery-dependent delay (Sun et al. 1995 recovery term) | reduced-order |
| SA / junctional / ventricular pacemakers, ectopic foci | Imposed cycle lengths with overdrive reset (foci: protected, not reset) | schematic automaticity |
| ECG | Lead-field forward model (infinite homogeneous medium) for chest leads; Einthoven lead vectors on the heart dipole for limb leads; scalar torso gains | reduced-order volume conductor |
| Great vessels in the 3-D view | Geometry for orientation only | illustration (no electrical role) |

Local APD90 of a cell is not the surface QT: the QT spans earliest ventricular activation to latest ventricular recovery across the heart.

## Known deviations from human data

- **PA interval** (~47 ms) is longer than the reference ~28 ± 7 ms.
- **ECG amplitudes (recalibrated in v1.4.0).** Until v1.3 the QRS was 2–3× too large for the T wave: lead I R 1.83 mV and V2–V3 S 2.7 mV against population medians of 0.75 and 0.94 mV (Cleveland Family Study, visit 5, n = 732; a sleep-apnoea family cohort, not a healthy-reference population). At tachycardia rates the deep V1–V3 complexes with tall T waves made supraventricular rhythms look ventricular. Two calibrated values changed: the limb-lead torso factor 2.5 → 1.0 (lead I R now 0.73 mV) and the chest half-depth 105 → 125 mm, i.e. the chest electrodes sit 20 mm further from the idealised heart (V2 S now 1.6 mV). The precordial gain (0.8) is unchanged because it already sat at the log-least-squares optimum for the six available medians. What still misfits: **R-wave progression is poor** (V3 R 0.42 vs 0.71 mV, V5 R 0.54 mV, transition at V5–V6), V2–V3 S remain ≈ 1.7× the median, and V6 T is flat. Re-orienting the heart (anterior tilt 15–60°, rotation about the long axis ±40°, frontal angle 45–65°) moved the transition to V3–V4 in some combinations but always inverted the lateral T waves and shrank lead II, so the anatomy was left unchanged. The fits are in `scripts/dev/geom-fit.ts` and `scripts/dev/chest-geom.ts`.
- **RBBB:** QRS ≈ 145 ms with terminal anterior/rightward forces, but the frontal area-based axis is distorted (≈ −140°). Status: *incomplete*.
- **LBBB:** QRS ≈ 140 ms (meets the width part of the Strauss et al. 2011 criterion, doi:10.1016/j.amjcard.2010.11.010) after introducing a nominal retrograde Purkinje–muscle junction delay (40 ms). Mid-QRS notching is not assessed.
- **Retrograde Purkinje entry:** myocardium → Purkinje conduction uses a separate nominal delay (40 ms) so that ectopic beats and LBBB spread mainly cell-to-cell (broad QRS). It was calibrated to QRS widths, not measured.
- **Left anterior fascicular block** does not produce left-axis deviation in this geometry.
- **RV mass** ≈ 26 % of ventricular cells (relatively high).
- **APD restitution (revised in Phase 2b):** APD = APD_ref · S(CL_mem) · (1 − 0.35·e^(−DI/50 ms)). S reproduces the human steady-state slope (23 ms per 100 ms CL, Franz et al. 1988, doi:10.1172/JCI113706); the fast term and the per-cell memory (weight 0.25 per beat, i.e. adaptation over ≈ 4–8 beats instead of the minutes reported in humans) are nominal shape choices. The previous single map produced unphysiologically short APDs (≈ 135 ms) for premature beats. S below CL 400 ms is an extrapolation.
- **Autonomic coefficients, nodal and pathway parameters** are nominal (marked "nominal" in the parameter panel); only directions of effect are meaningful.
- **Ectopic and focal rhythms:** automaticity is imposed by a rate/coupling parameter, not generated by ionic currents.
- **Re-entry** works through lumped links (AVNRT, AVRT). The pathways are joined at the His bundle; there is no lower common pathway.
- **Atrial flutter:** the circuit uses an idealised spherical RA with non-conducting tricuspid-annulus, caval and intercaval regions and a slow CTI (CV × 0.2, APD × 1.3; nominal). Cycle length ≈ 180 ms (≈ 330/min). The circuit runs **counterclockwise** (the RA septum activates inferior → superior), but the flutter waves in II/III/aVF are positive-dominant instead of the typical negative saw-tooth. The surface polarity is the resultant of the descending lateral RA (positive in the inferior leads) and the ascending septum plus left atrium (negative) (Ndrepepa et al., Z Kardiol 2000, doi:10.1007/s003920070225); in counterclockwise flutter the LA is entered mainly through the coronary-sinus ostium (Marine et al., Circulation 2001, doi:10.1161/hc3501.095478). Here the LA lies against the RA septum over a broad interface and is activated almost simultaneously, so the negative LA contribution is too weak. Adding an explicit coronary-sinus bridge did not change it, so it was not kept. Positive inferior F waves do occur in a minority of real counterclockwise flutters (13 % in Yan et al., Heart Vessels 2009, doi:10.1007/s00380-008-1108-z), but they are not the typical pattern.
- **AVNRT (recalibrated in v1.4.0):** the retrograde fast pathway was too slow (His → atrium ≈ 140 ms), putting a separate retrograde P wave in the ST segment at 153/min. Retrograde nodal time 60 → 20 ms, retrograde ERP 180 → 150 ms and slow-pathway AH 200 → 210 ms now give CL 340 ms (176/min) with atrial activation at the end of the QRS (pseudo-S in II), against typical slow–fast AVNRT CL 317 ± 43 ms and HA 45.8 ± 7.7 ms (Katritsis et al., Europace 2015, doi:10.1093/europace/euu387). Cycle lengths near 320 ms were reached only by also shortening the slow-pathway ERP, which moved atrial activation back out of the QRS (VA 44–68 ms), because a faster circuit leaves the fast pathway less recovery time and the retrograde delay grows (`scripts/dev/avnrt-scan*.ts`). The retrograde P is negative in V1 here; a pseudo-r′ in V1 is not reproduced.
- **Atrial fibrillation:** driven by a pulmonary-vein focus at 450/min (CL 133 ms; the median shortest PV CL in CAPLA was 126 ms, doi:10.1016/j.jacep.2023.08.018) in atria with seeded APD heterogeneity (SD 40 %) and CV 0.45 m/s. The atrial reference APD (300 ms) is calibrated so the emergent RA cycle length (≈ 170–240 ms) is near reported AF cycle lengths (CS AFCL 171 ± 27 ms, Verma et al. 2008, doi:10.1016/j.hrthm.2007.09.027). Wavelet dynamics in an event-driven graph (no source–sink or curvature effects) are qualitative. The irregular ventricular response relies on a nominal concealed-conduction rule in the AV node.
- **Scenario status:** since v1.2.0 no scenario is unsupported; scar VT, polymorphic VT, VF, calcium, EAD/DAD and the drug scenarios are *partial*, torsades and RBBB *incomplete*. See `docs/scenario-registry.json` and the sections below.

## Ionic model specifics (TP06)

- Equations transcribed from the gotran/CellML translation in cbcbeat (`tentusscher_panfilov_2006_epi_cell.py`, `_M_cell.py`); epicardial and M cells differ only in g_Ks (0.392 vs 0.098).
- **Endocardial variant entered from the published description, not from a machine-readable file:** g_to = 0.073; s_inf = 1/(1+exp((V+28)/5)); τ_s = 1000·exp(−(V+67)²/1000) + 8. The endocardial and M-cell initial conditions are approximate. Pacing for 20–30 beats reaches only approximate steady state (slow [Na⁺]i drift).
- **Calcium — the cell model is deliberately not used for the calcium scenarios.** In our runs TP06 lengthens APD when [Ca²⁺]o rises and shortens it when [Ca²⁺]o falls — the opposite of the clinical QT–calcium relationship. Measured here at CL 1000 ms, 30 beats (`scripts/dev/cao.ts`):

  | [Ca²⁺]o (mM) | 1.0 | 1.5 | 2.0 | 2.5 | 3.0 |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | APD90 epi (ms) | 255 | 283 | 306 | 324 | 337 |
  | APD90 M (ms) | 301 | 351 | 393 | 426 | 451 |
  | APD90 endo (ms) | 245 | 274 | 299 | 317 | 331 |

  Bartolucci et al. (Front Physiol 2020;11:314) show this is a general defect of models whose L-type calcium-dependent inactivation sits in the saturated part of its sensitivity curve, including O'Hara–Rudy and Grandi. The hyper- and hypocalcaemia scenarios therefore take the **size and sign of the tissue effect from human measurements** (30.1 ms of QT per mmol/L of total serum calcium; Young et al. 2021, Mendelian randomisation in UK Biobank, n = 76 226), not from the ionic model. The calcium slider remains in the Ionic cell tab so the disagreement is visible rather than hidden, and a regression test pins both facts. The slope is linear and was measured in populations near the reference range, so the scenarios at 1.6 and 3.2 mmol/L are extrapolations and probably **understate** the true change: the equivalent slope against *ionised* calcium in haemodialysis patients is roughly threefold steeper (8.55 ms per 0.1 mmol/L; Kim et al. 2019).
- **EADs and DADs — reproduced, but only after modifying the cell model.** Blocking IKr alone produces no EADs in TP06 at any degree of block (epi APD90 231 → 286 ms from gKr 0.5 → 0, no EAD). EADs appear only with the published modification that shortens the ICaL f-gate time constant and so enlarges the window current (τ_f × 0.5 with ICaL × 2.5 and IKs × 0.2: APD90 815 ms with a secondary upstroke at 393 ms; Vandersickel et al. 2014). DADs likewise do not arise from calcium loading alone; a background RyR leak must be added (leak 1.8 × 10⁻⁴ ms⁻¹ with ICaL × 2, SERCA × 4.5, NCX × 2.5 gives a triggered beat at 403 ms at CL 600 ms). **These are modifications of the model, not emergent properties of it**, and the scenario and UI text say so. The afterdepolarisations exist only in the single cell: the tissue receives their measured *timing* as an ectopic stimulus, because the ionic model is not coupled cell-to-cell.
- **Drug block is static.** Drug scenarios use the measured IC50 values of the CiPA ion-channel panel (Crumb et al. 2016) with the Hill equation at an assumed Hill coefficient of 1 (the source reports IC50 but not Hill coefficients for these channels). Use-dependence, state-dependent binding, trapping, metabolites and protein-binding variation are not modelled, and TP06 has no late sodium current — so late-INa blockers (ranolazine, mexiletine) are deliberately absent rather than modelled misleadingly. The torsade-risk category shown for each drug is clinical knowledge attached to the scenario text, **not a model output**: this model cannot predict whether a drug causes torsades.
- **Tissue mapping:** ionic results reach the tissue only as an APD scale (mean APD90 of epi/M/endo cells) and a CV scale (√ of the dV/dt_max ratio, from cable theory). The tissue AP *shape* is fixed, so:
  - a peaked T wave from faster repolarisation (hyperkalaemia) is not transferred;
  - U waves are not representable;
  - use-dependent Na⁺-channel block is not modelled;
  - Ca²⁺-channel-blocker effects on the AV node are not modelled.

## Potassium (recalibrated in v1.5.0)

APD, conduction velocity and AP amplitude follow the TP06 cell model at the given [K⁺]o (table in
`src/simulation/cellular/electrolytes.ts`, recomputed by `tests/physiology/ionic.test.ts`); conduction is
now scaled in atria, His–Purkinje system and ventricles, which lengthens PR and QRS and changes the P wave.
The **AP shape is calibrated**, not emergent. Mapped onto this tissue, TP06's own shape change barely
alters the ECG: at 8 mM the T wave grew ≈ 20 % and did not narrow; at 3 mM no U wave appeared
(`scripts/dev/k-try*.ts`). So:

- phase-3 width = baseline × (TP06 width ratio)^γ, with γ = 3.6 above and 2.4 below 5.4 mM;
- below 5.4 mM a slow terminal repolarisation component (up to 45 % of the plateau, delayed by up to
  30 % of the APD) inscribes the U wave — U waves arise from small voltage differences between AP tails
  (Ritsema van Eck et al. 2005); its size was set so that at 3.0 mM the U wave exceeds the flattened
  T wave (Marriott's "T–U reversal"), and it fuses with the T wave at milder levels.

What this does and does not reproduce:
- Hyperkalaemia 7.0 mM: T 1.35–1.6× taller in V2–V4, T slope/amplitude ratio up ≈ 10–25 % (the dialysis
  relation of Corsi et al. 2017 implies ≈ × 2 between 5.4 and 8 mM — not reached, because T-wave width
  here is set mainly by the spatial dispersion of repolarisation, which K⁺ barely changes in TP06),
  PR +20 ms, QRS +20 ms, P lower.
- Hyperkalaemia 8.5 mM: PR ≈ 218 ms, QRS ≈ 150 ms, P only ≈ 16 % lower — **P-wave loss (atrial
  standstill) is not reproduced**: atrial myocytes are more K⁺-sensitive than ventricular ones, and only a
  ventricular cell model is available. The wide QRS brings biphasic T waves in II and V5–V6, because this
  engine has no coupling between activation time and APD that would partly compensate the slower
  activation. Sine-wave patterns are not reproduced.
- Hypokalaemia 3.0 mM: flat T, prominent U wave larger than T in V2–V4, larger P wave, QT(U) longer.
  ST depression is not reproduced; PR does not lengthen (TP06 conduction is slightly faster).
- Clinically the K⁺–ECG relationship is weak (T-wave amplitude r ≤ 0.2 with serum K⁺ in routine ECGs,
  Kim et al. 2018; hyperkalaemic changes in only ≈ 46 % of patients at 6–9.3 mmol/L). The scenarios show
  the textbook pattern, not a predicted dose–response.
- 5.4 mmol/L is TP06's reference [K⁺]o and lies at the upper end of the normal serum range.

## Drugs (recalibrated in v1.5.0)

Dofetilide, quinidine and verapamil are now calibrated to a randomised human study at clinical doses
(Johannesen et al., Clin Pharmacol Ther 2014;96:549, n = 22, placebo-corrected): the model reproduces
ΔQTc, ΔJ–Tpeak, ΔTpeak–Tend and ΔPR within the published 95 % CIs (QT +78, +80, +4 ms; PR +32 ms for
verapamil). The previous TP06-derived scenarios were wrong against these data: hERG block prolonged QT
about three times too little, and verapamil *shortened* QT by ≈ 80 ms where man shows no significant
change (its clinical effect is PR prolongation, 32 ms). The calibration uses a uniform APD shift, a
slower phase 3 and, for verapamil, a longer AV-nodal conduction time. T-wave notching under dofetilide
and quinidine is not reproduced. Flecainide still uses the TP06 mapping (no matched human study here).
The generic "IKr inhibition" scenarios also still use TP06 and therefore under-predict QT prolongation.

## Acute ischaemia (new in v1.5.0)

- Territories are the standard AHA 17-segment vessel assignment (Cerqueira et al. 2002) on the idealised
  heart, extended to Marriott's seven culprit risk areas (Table 8.2); real coronary anatomy varies.
- AP changes at the centre of the territory: APD × 0.895, conduction × 0.448 (grade 3 only), resting
  potential +15.5 mV — TP06 under phase-1a conditions (Ko 9.9 mM, INa and ICaL × 0.85). The APD change
  matches human MAPs during LAD balloon occlusion (× 0.91, Taggart et al. 1996).
- The **AP-amplitude loss is calibrated** (× 0.655 instead of TP06's × 0.781) so that the mean of the
  proximal- and mid-LAD maximal ST shifts equals the LAD median of 1-minute balloon occlusion
  (0.26 mV, Haeberlin et al. 2014). The RCA (0.20 mV in III) and LCx (0.04 mV in V6) values of the same
  study are reproduced without further tuning (0.15/0.12 and 0.08 mV). TP06 lacks IK,ATP.
- Lead patterns of all seven culprit areas follow Table 8.2, with one deliberate exception: for a
  proximal RCA lesion the model shows ST *elevation* in V1 ≥ V2 (right-ventricular involvement, as in
  Marriott's text and Fig. 8.8), not the V1–V3 depression listed in the table.
- The maximal ST shift of LAD ischaemia falls in V4–V5, where human data put it in V3 — a consequence
  of the model's poor precordial R-wave progression.
- ST elevation in V4R with a proximal RCA lesion is ≈ 0.06 mV, below the clinical 0.1 mV criterion for
  RV infarction; the idealised RV free wall is thin.
- Only the acute phase: no necrosis, Q waves, T-wave inversion, reperfusion or evolution, and no
  subendocardial (ST-depression) ischaemia.

## Infarct phases (new in v1.6.0)

`ischaemia.phase` carries the territory through hyperacute → acute → evolving → subacute → old, with
three independent weights (`src/simulation/core/ischaemia.ts`): how much of the ischaemic action-potential
change remains (ST elevation, tall T), how much of the territory is necrotic, and how much extra APD
the surviving tissue carries (T inversion). The sequence follows Marriott ch. 9: "As the process of
ischemia transitions to infarction, T waves decrease in amplitude (later becoming negative); Q waves
develop; and ST segments decrease in amplitude."

How necrosis is represented, and why:
- Dead myocardium generates no action potential, so the forces that used to come from it are missing
  and the initial QRS vector points away from the infarct — the Q wave. It spreads outwards from the
  subendocardium (the wavefront phenomenon, Reimer & Jennings, Circulation 1977;56:786): a node dies
  when its depth from the endocardium is within necrosis × its ischaemic weight, so at full necrosis
  the core is transmural while the border zone stays subendocardial.
- Dead nodes are **removed from the lead field**, not merely given zero amplitude. Zeroing a region's
  amplitude leaves Σ W ≠ 0 over the surviving tissue, which puts a square-wave offset on the whole
  cardiac cycle — the first attempt did exactly that. Dropping every edge that touches a dead node keeps
  the zero-sum property, which is the condition that makes uniformly depolarised tissue silent.
- Dead tissue still conducts, at CV × 0.3. Removing it from the conducting graph as well gave a 450 ms
  QRS, because the necrotic subendocardium carries the Purkinje–muscle junctions for the whole
  territory. Slow conduction stands for the surviving subendocardial Purkinje fibres and intramural
  strands that keep an old infarct electrically continuous.

What it reproduces, measured on the simulated ECG (proximal LAD, `scripts/dev/infarct-phases.ts`):
hyperacute — tall T (V3 +6.1 mm) with ST only +0.5 to +1.2 mm; acute — ST maximal (V4 +2.1 mm, aVL
+1.1 mm) with reciprocal depression (III −1.5 mm); evolving — Q waves appear (V4 −6.4 mm, aVL −3.1 mm),
ST falls to +0.6 mm, T falls; subacute — Q waves with an isoelectric ST and inverted T over the infarct
(V4 −1.4 mm, aVL −1.1 mm); old — Q waves alone, T upright again.

Limits:
- **Timings are labels, not a process.** The model has no clock for healing: a phase is a state you
  select. The weights are nominal interpolations of the described sequence.
- **Q waves do not appear in V2–V3**, where an anterior infarct should show them most clearly. Those
  leads are dominated by a deep S in this model's poor R-wave progression (see "ECG amplitudes"), so
  there is no R for the infarct to remove.
- T-wave inversion reaches the leads directly over the infarct (V4, I, aVL) but not V2–V3, for the same
  reason. The peri-infarct APD boost needed to invert the T at all is 150 ms, which also stretches QT to
  about 490 ms in the subacute phase — longer than a typical healing infarct.
- No reperfusion, no evolution in time, no ventricular aneurysm (persistent ST elevation), no
  subendocardial (non-Q-wave) infarction as a separate entity.

## Extra chest leads (new in v1.5.0)

V3R–V6R are the mirror positions of V3–V6; V7 (posterior axillary), V8 (mid-scapular, 80 mm from the
midline) and V9 (left paraspinal, 30 mm) lie on the back of the idealised elliptical chest at the V6
level. The back positions are nominal. All extra leads use the same infinite-medium lead field as V1–V6.

## Measurement rules (arrhythmia-aware)

- **PR** is reported only when the model confirms that the P wave and the QRS belong to the same antegradely conducted impulse. It is suppressed for:
  - ventricular-origin complexes;
  - escape complexes;
  - re-entrant/echo complexes.
  The reason is shown per beat.
- For **QRS ≥ 120 ms** a note recommends JT (QT − QRS); JT is reported.
- **Rhythm-level analysis:** atrial activity is read from the model's activation records at three atrial sites (high RA, low RA, posterior LA) over 6 s. When the atrial cycle length is < 250 ms (an app display rule, not a diagnostic criterion):
  - regular activity (CV < 6 %) is reported as a flutter-wave rate with the AV conduction ratio;
  - irregular activity is reported as fibrillatory, and the table shows the mean ventricular rate and RR variability instead of a single RR; PR is not shown.
- **Detector:** QRS offset at 2.5 % of the peak spatial slope (captures slow terminal forces); onset extended to the PR-segment minimum of the spatial magnitude for delta waves.
- **Detector limits:**
  - For broad ectopic complexes and fast wide-complex tachycardia, the detected QRS end can merge with the T wave. Model truth (first to last ventricular activation) is shown alongside.
  - A dissociated P wave falling on a QRS can widen the detected QRS.
- **Delta-wave onset** is detected from the rise of the spatial ECG magnitude above its PR-segment minimum.
- Values are reported to the resolution of the sampling interval (2 ms at 500 Hz). Model "truth" values are internal activation/recovery times, not ECG readings.

## Ventricular re-entry: what sets the cycle length

This engine spreads activation as discrete events on a graph with refractoriness. It has no
source–sink mismatch, no wavefront curvature and no electrotonic coupling, and the consequence is
specific and measurable: **functional re-entry has no excitable gap**, so a rotor's period is just
the refractory period,

    CL(re-entry) ≈ erpRatio × apdFloor × APDref

Measured across 18 fibrillation runs at reference APDs of 300–380 ms (`scripts/dev/vf-scan2.ts`),
the ratio CL/APDref was **0.408–0.422** against a predicted 0.414. Three things follow.

- **Fibrillation cycle length is a calibration, not a prediction.** With the default floor the
  model fibrillates at ≈ 150 ms, far faster than human VF. The VF scenario raises the floor to 0.79
  so the local activation interval is 220 ms, against 218 ± 24 ms measured in vivo in humans
  (Nair et al. 2012) and a dominant frequency of 4.5–6.4 Hz (Bradley et al. 2011). The cost is that
  physiological APD shortening at short diastolic intervals is suppressed.
- **Anatomical re-entry has to be protected from functional re-entry.** The scar scenarios raise
  the floor to 0.60 so that faster functional rotors cannot outrun the circuit around the scar.
- **A premature beat cannot break a wavefront here.** Single-extrastimulus initiation failed in all
  30 R-on-T combinations tried (coupling 280–400 ms × dispersion 0.15–0.25 × two sites). Every
  re-entrant scenario therefore needs burst pacing, whereas clinically a single R-on-T beat often
  suffices.

## M cells (new in v1.6.0)

Until v1.5 the ventricular APD varied monotonically across the wall (endocardium longest, epicardium
shortest). Real ventricle has a midmyocardial (M) band that repolarises last, and that band matters
here for two reasons: it is what the Tpeak–Tend interval measures (Yan & Antzelevitch, Circulation
1998;98:1928), and it is the substrate the torsades literature invokes. `ventricles.mCellBoost` now adds
a Gaussian hump across the wall (centred at transmural 0.45) multiplied by Gaussians around the
circumference and along the long axis, so the longest-repolarising tissue forms an **island** in the LV
lateral wall rather than a layer (`src/simulation/core/mcells.ts`).

Calibration and limits:
- At the default 22 ms the transmural gradient through the island is 1.3 ms/mm with the model's 4 mm
  electrotonic smoothing, against 2.7 ± 0.9 ms/mm measured in control canine wedges (Akar et al.,
  Circulation 2002;105:1247). Reducing the smoothing radius to 1 mm gives 3.0 ms/mm. The smoothing
  radius is nominal, so the model sits at or just below the low end of the control range.
- The LQT2 wedges in the same study reach 12.2 ± 2.1 ms/mm. This model cannot: on a 2.5 mm grid with
  electrotonic smoothing the steepest gradient reachable is about 5 ms/mm at a plausible boost, and
  about 10 ms/mm only with the smoothing turned down to 1 mm and a boost of 140 ms.
- Tpeak–Tend rises from 60 to 70 ms as the smoothing radius falls from 4 to 1 mm; the reported human
  range is wider than either. The island's spatial widths are nominal — the wedge studies give a
  transmural profile, not a 3-D density map.

## Torsades de pointes: why the mechanism is imposed (re-examined in v1.6.0)

The question asked of v1.6 was whether torsades could be made to EMERGE from the substrate instead of
being driven by a scripted source. It cannot, and the reason is now measured rather than asserted.

**What was tried.** On a long-QT substrate (APD × 1.3) with the new M-cell island, a premature beat was
delivered from the epicardium directly over the island — the site with the shortest refractory period
next to the longest — across coupling intervals from 300 to 480 ms in 5–20 ms steps, with the island
amplitude at 80–160 ms, the electrotonic smoothing at 1–4 mm, conduction at 0.7–1.0 × normal, the APD
floor at 0.35–0.45, and the island widened from its default (55° circumference, 0.22 of the long axis)
up to a band covering essentially the whole free wall. Two-stimulus cross-field initiation (a second
stimulus 60–140 ms later at 90° and 135° around a ring of radius 14–22 mm) was tried as well.

**What happens.** Capture is all-or-nothing. At 415 ms the stimulus site is itself refractory and
nothing propagates; at 435 ms the entire ventricle is captured. There is no coupling interval at which
part of the ventricle is captured and part is left as a block line. The reason is a timing mismatch that
the model can be asked for directly: after a sinus beat the ventricle goes from 96 % refractory at
374 ms to 0 % at 514 ms, so the recovery front sweeps the whole chamber in about 140 ms, whereas a
premature wavefront needs 250–280 ms to cross it. Whatever is refractory when a premature beat starts
has recovered before the wavefront can reach it. Widening the island does not help, because the wave
simply travels around it and arrives from the far side later still.

**Why that is structural.** Functional re-entry needs an obstacle that persists for a lap. In a real
heart that obstacle is the rotor core, held unexcitable by the curvature of the wavefront itself
(source–sink mismatch). This engine is an event-driven graph: it has conduction and refractoriness but
no diffusion, so it has no curvature and cannot hold a core. The only obstacles it can offer are
anatomical — which is exactly why atrial flutter and scar VT, which circulate around fixed obstacles,
do work here and are *validated*. A dispersion-created obstacle could in principle persist if its
refractory period exceeded the tachycardia cycle length, but that is circular: it requires the
tachycardia to already be running.

There is also a resolution limit underneath this. Block persists only where the repolarisation gradient
is steeper than the conduction time over the same distance. The LQT2 wedge gradient of 12.2 ms/mm gives
122 ms of dispersion across a 10 mm wall, crossed in about 17 ms. On this 2.5 mm grid, after electrotonic
smoothing, the steepest representable gradient is 3–10 ms/mm, so the margin is at best marginal
and the wave's detour around the block line erases it.

**Conclusion.** Torsades stays an imposed source, and the scenario says so. The experiments are in
`scripts/dev/tdp-emergent.ts`, `tdp-block.ts`, `tdp-refrac.ts` and `tdp-rotor.ts`, so the negative
result can be re-run. Reproducing it properly needs a monodomain reaction–diffusion model on a
sub-millimetre mesh, which is a different simulator, not a parameter change.

### Torsades de pointes: which mechanism, and what is reproduced

Torsades was first attempted as **re-entry**, and that route failed:

1. **Initiation** — a late-coupled R-on-T beat on a long-QT, dispersed substrate never produced
   re-entry in any of 30 combinations (coupling 280–400 ms × dispersion 0.15–0.25 × two sites). A
   single premature beat cannot break a wavefront in this engine.
2. **Morphology** — when re-entry was instead started with a burst, the axis rotated at chance
   consistency, i.e. randomly polymorphic.
3. **Rate** — those rhythms ran at 166–200 ms, fibrillation rates rather than torsades rates.

Dessertenne's **two competing foci** was tried next and also failed on the waveform: it produced a
single fade rather than repeating spindles (lead II peak-to-peak 1.82 → 0.24 mV, then flat), with
narrow spiky complexes, because the two foci only capture part of the ventricle at that rate. A
source that always moved to the globally best-recovered site locked into a two-site ping-pong.

The scenario therefore uses a **drifting source**, a reduced-order stand-in for the drifting rotor
that optical-mapping and simulation work holds responsible for the undulating axis (a Doppler effect;
reviewed in Front Cardiovasc Med 2024;11:1363848).

#### What changed in v1.7.0, and why

An independent review of v1.5.0 found three things wrong with how that source was built. All three
were reproduced on v1.6.0 before being changed.

**The source repeated sites and then jumped.** The position was a fractional index that was then
rounded, so at drift 0.6 on a 16-site ring the sequence was `0 1 1 2 2 3 4 4 5 5 …`. Worse, the sites
were spaced by anatomical angle, which is not the same as the axis each produces: measured on this
geometry, stepping one site changed the frontal QRS axis by anything from −2° to +67°.

The source path is now annotated with the axis each site produces, and the source advances a fixed
increment of AXIS per beat, walking as far along the path as that takes (`advanceIndex`). The
annotation is the sum of the lead field's dipole weights over the tissue within one conduction radius
(35 mm) of the site — the heart vector while the QRS is still growing. Checked against beats actually
simulated from every site on the path, that predicts the realised frontal axis to a median of 8°
(90th percentile 37°). The single node's own lead-field weight does *not* predict it: on a voxel grid
those weights quantise to multiples of 45°.

The path itself stays ordered **anatomically**, not by axis. Ordering it by axis was tried and makes
consecutive sites spatially unrelated, so the wavefront never reaches the next site and the run dies:
11 beats over 1.8 s, against 58 with the anatomical order. Spatial continuity is what keeps the chain
alive; the adaptive step size is what makes the twist even.

Residual: the realised twist is 29 ± 17° per beat against 18° requested, because the path's angular
resolution is limited — some anatomically adjacent sites differ by as much as 68° in the axis they
produce, so the walk cannot always stop close to the requested increment.

**Disabling the generator did not stop a run.** The whole run was scheduled in advance, so switching
it off prevented future scheduling only; measured on v1.6.0, fifteen further stimuli fired in the four
seconds after disabling mid-episode. Reading the code while reproducing that also showed the episode
state was absent from the snapshot, so a checkpoint taken mid-episode replayed differently (28
stimuli against 0).

The generator is now an explicit state machine (`src/simulation/core/torsades.ts`) whose every queued
event carries an epoch token. Disabling it, or changing any of its parameters, increments the token
and voids everything already queued. The state is part of the snapshot. Both are tested
(`tests/physiology/torsadesEpisode.test.ts`).

**The run did not depend on the substrate.** The same generator produced episodes with normal APD
(measured: 60 stimuli in 30 s), so the scenario could not support any statement about long QT.

The triggered beat now arises at the site with the longest action potential — in practice the M-cell
island — and is **gated** on that action potential still being in phase 3 at the takeoff time, which
is what an EAD needs. On a normal-QT substrate nothing fires (0 stimuli in 30 s, against 20 on the
long-QT substrate). Because that cell is itself refractory when the EAD takes off, the wavefront
emerges in the nearest recovered tissue, which is why the first complex of an episode starts near but
not at the long-QT zone.

This is a threshold on APD, not a model of EAD dynamics in tissue, and it must not be read as a
torsade-risk prediction for a drug.

**One further defect, found while fixing the above.** The paired counter-rotating loop and the
propagation hook could both advance the state machine, so each beat spawned two and the chain branched
exponentially — six stimuli inside 33 ms, fusing into one complex and making the run look
monomorphic. Only the source's own excitation now advances the chain, and the second loop has its own
event code that never touches the state machine. It is off by default in v1.7.0: with the source
advancing evenly it is no longer needed to keep the amplitude from vanishing.

#### What makes the complexes merge

Successive activations must overlap, or the tracing is a train of discrete spikes rather than a
continuous undulation. Two parameters are set for the run, and both are nominal rather than
calibrated: the APD floor 0.45 → 0.5, which sets the period of the circulating source, and the depth
of CV restitution 0.5 → 0.85. At the short diastolic intervals of the run the latter slows conduction
to about 39 % of normal, so each activation takes 229 ms against a 273 ms cycle. Conduction velocity
does fall steeply at short diastolic intervals near the point of block, so the direction is right; the
size is a tuning choice made to obtain the appearance.

Two criteria remain outside target: the complexes are still more fragmented than a real recording, and
the envelope turns over more often than a clinical spindle. The envelope swing is smaller than v1.6's,

complexes spiky.

What the scenario does **not** do is explain why a long QT predisposes to torsades. The substrate gate
decides whether the generator may fire; it does not generate the arrhythmia from the prolonged
repolarisation. The experiments showing why it cannot be made emergent are in the section above, and a
staged route to a model that could is in `docs/MECHANISTIC_TDP_PROPOSAL.md`.

Two measurement notes. The analyser treats activations within 80 ms as one fused QRS; without that,
two foci firing 8 ms apart were counted as two beats, which doubled the apparent rate and turned a
smooth rotation into a zigzag. And axis-rotation metrics alone were the wrong target: a single
126° sweep scores well on rotation consistency while looking nothing like torsades. What the eye
reads is the **amplitude envelope** of one lead, so that is now measured directly.

### QRS detection (rewritten in v1.7.0)

The ECG measurements are made by a detector that sees only the simulated signals, never the model's
internal state, so that the ECG ↔ activation correspondence can be checked rather than assumed. Until
v1.6.0 that detector compared the multi-lead spatial slope against **30 % of its maximum over the
whole analysed window**, which made detection non-causal: multiplying the last second of an
eight-second sinus recording by ten removed all seven complexes already detected in the first six
seconds, because the threshold rose with the loudest part of the record.

It is now the standard arrangement — adaptive threshold, refractory period, search-back
(Pan & Tompkins 1985) — applied to the multi-lead spatial slope rather than one filtered lead, so a
complex that is isoelectric in one lead still contributes:

- Threshold NPKI + 0.35 (SPKI − NPKI), with running signal and noise peak levels. A deflection that
  fails the threshold updates the NOISE level, so small deflections push the threshold **up**. An
  earlier version used the median of recent accepted peaks, which has the opposite behaviour: one
  accepted P wave lowered the estimate enough to admit the next, and the detector began reporting
  P waves as complexes for the rest of the record.
- 180 ms refractory period; search-back at half the threshold after 1.6 × the running RR, with
  search-back acceptances deliberately excluded from the signal-level update for the same reason.
- T-wave discrimination: within 360 ms of a complex a candidate must be at least 0.65 × as steep, or
  no broader than it. Without this the peaked T of severe hyperkalaemia (QRS ≈ 150 ms, tall
  symmetrical T) was counted as a second complex and the apparent rate doubled.

Measured against the simulator's own wave records across every scenario, the new detector finds
95.5 % of ventricular activations with 18 spurious detections, against 94.1 % and 42 for the rule it
replaced. Known limits:

- **Fibrillation is not detectable** and is excluded from the accuracy tests: there are no discrete
  complexes to find (4 of 33 activations matched). Rhythm-level measures are used there instead.
- Antidromic AVRT and fast focal VT lose a few complexes where activations fuse.
- In pre-excitation the detector marks onset at the delta wave, which is correct but shifts the
  reported onset about 78 ms earlier than the model's first ventricular activation.

### Comparison with real torsades recordings

The review that prompted v1.7.0 asked for comparison against independently sourced, appropriately
licensed reference recordings. **That has not been done**, and the reason is a practical one rather
than a judgement that it does not matter: this work runs in an offline environment with no access to
a licensed ECG archive, and reproducing a copyrighted tracing here would not be lawful. What has been
done instead is to compare against **published quantitative descriptions** — rate, the absence of an
isoelectric baseline, the envelope behaviour, the axis sweep, the activation span — which are the
numbers in the table above and the criteria coded in `src/simulation/ecg-forward/tdpMetrics.ts`.

Anyone with access to PhysioNet or a similar archive can close this gap: the metrics module takes a
twelve-lead signal and a window, so running it over real torsades episodes and comparing the
distributions is a direct extension. Two things should be expected to differ.

- **Real torsades is more variable.** The criteria here are a central tendency; clinical recordings
  include episodes that are slower than 180 /min, episodes with no visible twist in some leads, and
  episodes that degenerate into fibrillation. A simulated run that always meets every criterion is
  less variable than the real thing, and the criteria should not be read as necessary conditions for
  a recording to be torsades.
- **The rate here is set by refractoriness on an idealised substrate.** It is reproducible to the
  millisecond between runs, which no recording is.

### Rotation does not separate torsades from polymorphic VT here

Net rotation divided by total rotation ("straightness") cleanly separates organised polymorphic
rhythms from fibrillation — 0.89 for torsades and 0.94 for the polymorphic VT scenario, against
0.18 for fibrillation. It does **not** separate torsades from polymorphic VT: both rotate
coherently. In this model the two differ by setting (long QT with triggered foci versus a scar),
rate, and whether the rhythm self-terminates — not by the twist itself.

### Polymorphic VT versus fibrillation

The model does not draw a sharp line between them. The two scenarios differ in degree of
organisation and rate — axis dispersion ≈ 61° at ≈ 310 ms for polymorphic VT against ≈ 85° at
≈ 220 ms for fibrillation — rather than by distinct mechanisms.

### Baseline ECG in the scar scenarios

The slow border zone distorts the tracing before any arrhythmia is induced: in sinus rhythm the
monomorphic scar substrate gives QRS ≈ 112 ms with QT ≈ 560 ms, and the polymorphic one QRS ≈ 132 ms
with QT ≈ 506 ms. Some widening is expected after a large infarct, but these are at the extreme end
and are an artefact of applying one slowing factor to a 6 mm rim of tissue.
